# Daily Struggles of Neurodivergent Adults: Memory, Time, Task-Tracking, and Recall

Scope: working-age adults with ADHD, executive dysfunction, autism/AuDHD, and ADHD/autistic burnout. Written for designing Twinwire, an AI "alter ego" memory app. Research date: 2026-11-09. Source-quality labels: [PEER] peer-reviewed or preprint research; [GOV] government/agency data; [PRESS] university press release or news coverage; [VENDOR] app-company or clinic marketing content; [COMMUNITY] first-person or forum content.

---

## 1. Most common daily struggles tied to working memory and executive function

### Takeaway
Adults with ADHD show measurable, moderate-sized working memory deficits, and their long-term memory weakness comes mainly from information not being encoded well in the first place, not from failing to retrieve it later. The bigger everyday problems are planning, getting started, and sensing time. This fits a "memory fade" pattern where details of a conversation, a name, or a promise were never firmly stored. Autistic adults report a related but separate problem, "autistic inertia": they find it hard to start, stop, or switch activities.

### Cited Findings

**Working memory (WM)**
- [PEER] A meta-analysis of 38 studies (Alderson et al., 2013) found moderate between-group effect sizes for working memory deficits in adults with ADHD compared with controls. It suggested that differences in methods explain why some individual studies fail to detect WM deficits. — [Alderson et al. 2013, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23688211/)

**Long-term memory: encoding, not retrieval (directly relevant to the "3-day fade")**
- [PEER] Skodzik, Holling & Pedersen (2017), meta-analysis of 19 studies, *Journal of Attention Disorders* 21(4):267–283. Adults with ADHD did worse on verbal (not visual) long-term memory. The authors concluded that "memory deficits in adult ADHD reflect a learning deficit induced at the stage of encoding." The verbal long-term memory deficit was strongly tied to a deficit in acquiring the memory, and "no retrieval problems were observable." IQ did not explain the group differences. — [Skodzik et al. 2017, MetaTOC listing](https://metatoc.com/papers/28912-long-term-memory-performance-in-adult-adhd-a-meta-analysis); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/1087054713510561)
  - Effect size: one secondary review reports d = 0.49 for verbal long-term memory. I could not check this against the original abstract, so verify it before citing. A second summary says a visual-memory effect was driven mostly by one outlier study. — same sources as above (secondary summaries; unverified)

**Prospective memory (remembering to do things later, such as promises and follow-ups)**
- [PEER] Fuermaier et al. (2013) compared 45 unmedicated adults with ADHD with 45 matched controls on a complex prospective memory task. They found a **large impairment in task planning**, but only "negligible to small effects" for recalling the plan, starting it on one's own, and carrying it out. Inhibition contributed significantly to planning performance. — [Fuermaier et al. 2013, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23484020/)
- I found no meta-analysis focused specifically on prospective memory in adult ADHD (see Gaps).

**Time perception / "time blindness"**
- [PEER] A 2023 narrative review (Mette) concludes that time perception is impaired in adult ADHD. It also notes that adult studies are "very scarce" and the results are mixed: some studies find deficits in time estimation, reproduction, and management, and others find no clear link. The review attributes the mixed results to differences in diagnostic protocols and study methods. It describes the subjective feeling that time moves faster, which makes prospective timing tasks harder. — [Mette 2023, "Time Perception in Adult ADHD: Findings from a Decade — A Review," PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9962130/)
- [PEER] A meta-analysis of 55 studies (Marx et al., 2021; all ages, not adults only) found "meta-analytic evidence of a broad range of timing deficits" in people with ADHD. — [Marx et al. 2021, Southampton eprints](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/456003)
- [PEER] Participant quote from a 2026 CSCW interview study (n=22 ADHD adults): P5: "Unless I pay special attention, my perception of time is as if I want to deliberately forget it." The same study found that participants misjudged how long tasks would take and felt little urgency until deadlines became concrete. — [Chen, Meng & Nie 2026, arXiv/CSCW 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.17258v1)
- "Time blindness" itself is a community and clinical term. None of the peer-reviewed sources I found tested it under that name. — [Mette 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9962130/)

**Task initiation**
- [PEER] In the Chen et al. interviews, many participants knew what they had to do but could not start it, even routine things like getting out of bed. Some relied on "crisis-driven productivity," using deadline pressure to get moving, and recognized that this was exhausting and unsustainable. Plans made on rare high-focus days were often abandoned, which led to self-blame. — [Chen et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.17258v1)
- [PEER] P17: "My friend calls me to remind me to get up and do things. Without that, I might just stay in bed all day." P3: "If a friend or even the app tells me to go to the library, I can start." — [Chen et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.17258v1)

**Autism: autistic inertia (starting, stopping, switching)**
- [PEER] Buckle, Leadbitter, Poliakoff & Gowen (2021), *Frontiers in Psychology*, the first study of "autistic inertia," described as "a widespread and often debilitating difficulty acting on their intentions." Participants described difficulty starting, stopping, and changing activities that was "not within their conscious control." The paper's title quotes a participant: "No Way Out Except From External Intervention." The authors note the term may be an umbrella for problems with different causes, such as executive function differences or movement differences. — [Buckle et al. 2021, PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8314008/)
- [PRESS/academic commentary] The same forces that make starting hard can also make stopping overwhelming, and fear of failure can make transitions harder without external support. — [APS Observer Student Notebook, "The Physics of Autistic Inertia"](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/observer/student-notebook-autistic-inertia-srinivasan.html)

**Emotional dysregulation (amplifies every memory lapse into shame)**
- [PEER] A 2020 meta-analysis in *BMC Psychiatry* states that about 70% of adult ADHD patients report emotion dysregulation or emotional lability (citing earlier sources). — [BMC Psychiatry 2020](https://bmcpsychiatry.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/s12888-020-2442-7)
- [PEER] A 2023 systematic review (Soler-Gutiérrez et al.) argues that emotion dysregulation is a core symptom of adult ADHD. — [PubMed 36608036](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36608036/)
- [PEER] Shaw et al. (2014), *American Journal of Psychiatry*, reviewed how common emotion dysregulation is in ADHD. Its table shows rates such as 38% (2009 population study) and 47% (2011 clinic study). The "34–70%" range often attributed to this paper could not be confirmed. — [Shaw et al. 2014, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4282137)

### Inferences
- The encoding-deficit finding (Skodzik) suggests the user's "3-day memory fade" may be less about forgetting and more about never storing the memory firmly at the moment of the conversation. A tool that captures context at the moment of encoding (right after a conversation) addresses the actual mechanism. Spaced reminders alone would not.
- Fuermaier's result (planning is impaired much more than recalling the intention) suggests that "what did I promise?" may be easier to fix than "when and how do I do it?" Twinwire could turn a captured promise into a concrete next step with a time attached.
- The quotes from Chen et al. and Buckle et al. agree that an external prompt (a person, or something that acts like a person) gets people started where self-set reminders do not.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed research on **forgetting names** in ADHD or autistic adults specifically. Name and face recall in adult ADHD is not covered here.
- I found no research testing a specific **"3-day" decay window**. The "few days" pattern appears only in qualitative data (Chen P11, see Section 5).
- **"Object permanence" / "out of sight, out of mind"** is a community metaphor. No peer-reviewed study tests it in adult ADHD.
- **Decision paralysis** in adult ADHD/autism: I found no peer-reviewed source in this search.
- I found no pooled meta-analytic estimate for **prospective memory** in adult ADHD.
- The Metcalfe, McFeaters & Voyer (2023/2024) lifespan meta-analysis of time perception in ADHD was found but its adult-specific effect sizes were not retrieved.

## 2. How these struggles show up at work and in social life

### Takeaway
At work, the same deficits show up as trouble taking notes while following a meeting, email and notifications derailing focus, and deadlines that seem to arrive without warning. Socially, they show up as "involuntary ghosting": forgetting to reply, then avoiding the person out of shame. Both are made worse by masking, which takes effort and itself drains memory and focus. Most workplace and social evidence is practitioner or vendor content, not peer-reviewed research.

### Cited Findings

**Work**
- [VENDOR survey] 2 in 3 employees with ADHD say work is challenging on a daily basis. Respondents also reported problems with boredom, forgetfulness, and procrastination. This is a commercial blog survey, not peer-reviewed. — [Skynova, "ADHD in the Workplace"](https://www.skynova.com/blog/adhd-in-the-workplace)
- [Practitioner content] Working memory limits make it hard to take notes and follow a conversation at the same time in meetings. People with ADHD may interrupt because they fear they will forget their thought. An email notification can derail a project into a 15-minute inbox cleanup. Deadlines can "arrive suddenly" because time estimates left out steps. Sources disagree on notifications: some recommend electronic reminders for meetings and due dates, while others warn that constant digital notifications make the challenges worse. These points are aggregated from practitioner articles, so attribution to a single article is approximate. — [Simply Psychology, "ADHD Work Challenges"](https://www.simplypsychology.org/adhd-problems-at-work.html); [Understood.org, "8 common ways people struggle at work"](https://www.understood.org/en/articles/common-work-problems-and-causes)
- [PRESS, study 2026] Simon Fraser University study of 202 people in British Columbia (aged 16+) who reported a professional ADHD diagnosis, published in *Research in Neurodiversity* (lead author Marisa Mylett, co-author Grace Iarocci). More than 91% said they camouflage (mask) their ADHD traits to some extent. Masking was linked to exhaustion, anxiety, depression, and low self-esteem. The extra cognitive effort of masking "can also make core ADHD traits worse, impacting attention, memory and concentration." Masking may delay diagnosis and limit access to support. Common tactics included pretending, mimicking others, overpreparing, avoiding social situations, suppressing fidgeting or interrupting, and self-medicating. — [SFU media release, June 2026](https://www.sfu.ca/sfunews/media/media-releases/2026/06/adults-with-adhd-may-pay-high-price-to-mask-traits-and-fit-in--s.html); [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1130768)
- [PEER] Chen et al. describe a mismatch with institutions: workplace and productivity tools assume "steady self-regulation and linear time," and notification clutter adds to the burden. — [Chen et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17258)

**Social life**
- [VENDOR] "Involuntary ghosting" is described as "the disappearing act that happens when ADHDers forget to respond to texts." Forgetting turns into avoidance out of shame. Mechanisms cited include follow-through, time perception, and working memory. — [Inflow, "Texting with ADHD: 6 ways to stop ghosting your friends"](https://getinflow.io/post/adhd-involuntary-ghosting-texting-friends)
- [Clinician commentary] Friendships fading is "rarely about lack of interest." Many adults with ADHD carry guilt or shame and assume they are "bad friends," and that shame makes reaching out feel harder, which keeps the cycle going. — [Fastreat community Q&A](https://www.fastreat.com/community/posts/maintaining-friendships-adhd-adults); [Fastreat, "Why do friendships fade"](https://www.fastreat.com/community/posts/10064)
- [VENDOR] Suggested fixes include external reminders, friends who understand these patterns, and asking friends for a reminder the night before. — [Inflow, "ADHD and friendship"](https://www.getinflow.io/post/making-friends-with-adult-adhd)
- [PEER] Task management is relational. Participants used friends, body doubling, and shared routines to start and keep going. People with little social support carried more of the load alone. P13: "Only in the ADHD group do I feel that I can tell the truth." P6, on disclosure: "When I told my sister I had ADHD, she said, 'Then just correct it.'" — [Chen et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.17258v1)
- [PEER] Autistic adults in Raymaker et al. (2020) described a lack of empathy from neurotypical people, and burnout reduced their capacity for work and relationships (see Section 3). — [Raymaker et al. 2020, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7313636/)

### Inferences
- Missed follow-ups at work (after meetings) and social follow-ups (replying, keeping promises) are the same failure: an intention formed during a live interaction is never externalized. Twinwire's capture point is the end of the conversation.
- Shame is the multiplier. A missed reply becomes avoidance, which becomes a lost relationship. A tool that makes re-entry easy (for example, "here's what you last talked about and a low-pressure way to reply") targets the avoidance step, not just the forgetting step.
- Masking uses up the same working memory needed to remember. High-masking settings such as meetings and new social contacts are probably where encoding is weakest, so these are the settings where automatic capture adds the most.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed, large-sample data on how adults with ADHD handle email, meetings, or project tools specifically.
- A 2024 field study of 171 employees (attributed to Turjeman-Levi et al., possibly *AIMS Public Health*) reportedly found higher job burnout in workers with ADHD, mediated by executive function deficits. It surfaced only in secondary sources and I could not verify it.
- A 2024 study reportedly found higher camouflaging scores in adults with ADHD than in neurotypical comparison participants. The primary paper was not found.
- I found no peer-reviewed survey data on how often adults with ADHD forget to reply to messages.
- The SFU masking study details come from the press release, not the journal article.

## 3. ADHD and autistic burnout: what it is, what triggers it, and how it affects memory and daily functioning

### Takeaway
Autistic burnout is a defined, research-backed syndrome: chronic exhaustion, loss of skills (explicitly including memory and executive function), and lower tolerance for sensory input, typically lasting 3+ months and driven by masking and unmet demands. "ADHD burnout" is widely discussed but not formally defined. The closest evidence links ADHD masking to exhaustion and to worse memory and attention.

### Cited Findings

**Autistic burnout (Raymaker et al., 2020) — foundational**
- [PEER] Citation: Raymaker et al. (2020), "Having all of your internal resources exhausted beyond measure and being left with no clean-up crew": Defining autistic burnout, *Autism in Adulthood* 2(2):132–143. Community-based participatory research: 19 interviews (10 focused on burnout, 9 from an employment study in which burnout came up unprompted), 19 social media sources (5 analyzed in depth), and about 200 #AutisticBurnout tweets. — [Raymaker et al. 2020, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7313636/)
- [PEER] Definition: "a syndrome conceptualized as resulting from chronic life stress and a mismatch of expectations and abilities without adequate supports." It involves pervasive, long-term exhaustion (typically 3+ months), loss of function, and reduced tolerance to stimulus. — [Raymaker et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7313636/)
- [PEER] Three core features:
  1. **Chronic exhaustion**: physical, mental, emotional, or social depletion from overextending.
  2. **Loss of skills**: thinking, **memory**, planning, self-care, work, social, and emotional-regulation skills. Some participants never returned to their previous baseline.
  3. **Reduced stimulus tolerance**: more overload, meltdowns and shutdowns, and avoiding activities they used to enjoy.
  — [Raymaker et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7313636/)
- [PEER] Participant quotes on memory and executive function:
  - "I would lose executive function and self-care skills."
  - "...the really, really scary part of burnout is you don't know whether or not you're gonna get those skills back..."
  - A narrative list: "Decline in executive functioning...loss of life skills, poor memory, problems with speech..."
  - Memory is not a separate theme in the paper. It appears inside the skills-loss findings.
  — [Raymaker et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7313636/)
- [PEER] Triggers:
  - **Life stressors** that add up over time. Masking was the most prominent. Others were expectations, managing disability in environments without accommodations, and life changes; many first burnouts happened around the transition to adulthood.
  - **Barriers to support**: gaslighting and dismissal, difficulty setting boundaries or self-advocating, being unable to take breaks, and lack of resources (disability services, respite, diagnosis).
  - **Tipping point**: expectations outweigh abilities.
  - One participant described long-term masking as leaving "psychic plaque."
  — [Raymaker et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7313636/)
- [PEER] Impacts: mental and physical health, capacity for independent living (work, relationships, daily tasks), and quality of life. Some participants reported self-injury, suicidal thoughts, or suicide attempts. — [Raymaker et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7313636/)
- [PEER] What helped recovery: acceptance and social support (including connection with the autistic community); time off, rest, and lower expectations; unmasking and doing things in more autistic ways; accommodations; asking for help; setting boundaries; recognizing burnout early; earlier diagnosis. — [Raymaker et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7313636/)
- [PEER] The authors consider autistic burnout distinct from occupational burnout and clinical depression, though they can co-occur. Key depression features such as anhedonia and sleep problems were outliers in their data. Limitations: small convenience sample, mostly non-Hispanic white, few participants with high support needs. — [Raymaker et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7313636/)
- [Organization] The UK National Autistic Society maintains professional guidance on autistic burnout. — [National Autistic Society, "Understanding autistic burnout"](https://autism.org.uk/advice-and-guidance/professional-practice/autistic-burnout)

**ADHD burnout / masking-driven exhaustion**
- [PRESS, study 2026] SFU study (n=202): more than 91% mask their ADHD traits. Masking is linked to exhaustion, anxiety, depression, and low self-esteem, and the effort of masking can worsen attention, memory, and concentration. — [SFU media release](https://www.sfu.ca/sfunews/media/media-releases/2026/06/adults-with-adhd-may-pay-high-price-to-mask-traits-and-fit-in--s.html)
- [PEER] Chen et al. found that crisis-driven productivity (relying on deadline adrenaline) was described as exhausting and unsustainable. — [Chen et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.17258v1)
- [VENDOR/blog] Most "ADHD burnout" content online (signs, recovery steps) comes from app companies and clinics, not peer-reviewed research. — e.g., [Habi, "ADHD Burnout: 12 Signs"](https://habi.app/insights/adhd-burnout/); [Saner.ai, "ADHD burnout"](https://blog.saner.ai/adhd-burnout/)

### Inferences
- Burnout means memory and executive function capacity are not fixed. They can drop sharply for months. A memory tool must work for a user who is temporarily much less capable: less setup, fewer decisions, more done automatically. Burnout is when users most need the tool and are least able to maintain it.
- Raymaker's recovery factors (lower expectations, rest, accommodation) suggest an app "burnout / low-capacity mode" that cuts down prompts, pauses nonessential follow-ups, and protects only the most important relationships and commitments.
- Masking is both a trigger for burnout and a drain on memory. A tool that removes the need to "perform remembering" (for example, a quick pre-meeting brief on a person and your last conversation) may reduce masking load.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed formal definition or validated measure of "ADHD burnout."
- Higgins et al. (2021, Australian Delphi study) proposed a broader definition of autistic burnout that adds withdrawal, depersonalization, and masking. Arnold et al. (2023) reportedly found that 141 autistic adults endorsed it. Both surfaced only in search summaries; the primary papers were not fetched.
- I found no data on burnout specifically in AuDHD (co-occurring autism and ADHD) adults.
- I found no data on how often burnout episodes recur or how long they typically last beyond "3+ months."
- The commonly repeated claim that "62% of people with ADHD have chronic fatigue" has no identifiable study behind it and was excluded.

## 4. What neurodivergent people say helps, and what makes things worse

### Takeaway
In their own words, ADHD adults want support that comes to them rather than requiring them to open it. They want prompts that feel social and nonjudgmental, minimal setup, rest framed as care rather than failure, and AI that suggests rather than decides. What makes things worse: tools they have to maintain, reminders that become invisible, rigid schedules and static alarms, setup complexity, guilt-inducing overdue lists and streaks, and anything that feels like surveillance.

### Cited Findings

**What helps (from the Chen et al. 2026 interviews, n=22, and speed-dating study, n=20)**
- [PEER] P14: "I hope the system comes to me instead of me opening it." — [Chen et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.17258v1)
- [PEER] Social presence and co-regulation: participants valued "Social Presence AI," quiet, nonjudgmental companionship like a body double. Friends, body doubling, and shared routines were the most effective existing supports. — [Chen et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.17258v1)
- [PEER] A "Brain Weather Dashboard" (a metaphorical display of current mental capacity) felt less stigmatizing than overdue-task lists. — [Chen et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.17258v1)
- [PEER] "Emotionally Aware Pause Days" were appreciated because they framed rest as care rather than failure. "Flexible Planning with Gentle Streaks" (baseline goals) reduced self-criticism, though some worried about losing motivation. — [Chen et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.17258v1)
- [PEER] Recurring principle: participants wanted AI to **suggest rather than decide**, with user control over how often it nudges, its tone, and opt-in/opt-out. — [Chen et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.17258v1)
- [PEER] Autistic adults: external intervention is often what breaks inertia ("No Way Out Except From External Intervention"). — [Buckle et al. 2021](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8314008/)
- [PEER] Autistic burnout recovery: acceptance, community, lower expectations, unmasking, accommodations. — [Raymaker et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7313636/)
- [COMMUNITY] Workarounds people describe: setting habits to 6 days a week so one missed day doesn't break a streak; a tracker with no streaks and no penalty for missed days; a sticky notification that stays until acted on. These come from Reddit posts seen through third-party mirror snapshots; individual post attribution is unverified, and some posts are app promotion. — [Reddit mirror snapshot (example)](https://reddit.sentinel-team.org/posts/1rn77k0/snapshots/2026-03-13T19%3A10%3A13.708674Z)

**What makes things worse**
- [PEER] P11: "If it's only me reminding myself, it just becomes invisible after a few days." — [Chen et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.17258v1)
- [PEER] P4: "Many apps are too complicated to set up, and I feel more anxious using them." — [Chen et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.17258v1)
- [PEER] P10: "Every time I write a plan, I get anxious because I know I won't follow it." And: "I know it's ADHD, but I still feel like I should just try harder. Other people manage; why can't I?" — [Chen et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.17258v1)
- [PEER] Rigid schedules and static alarms made participants feel more isolated, not supported. — [Chen et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.17258v1)
- [PEER] Surveillance concerns. S14: "Even if I know it's trying to help, it feels like it's watching me." "Shared Planning with Trusted Persons" was seen by some as intrusive or surveillance-like. "Pattern-Based Nudges" split opinion: helpful to some, intrusive to others. — [Chen et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.17258v1)
- [PEER] Friction at the wrong moment: an "Emotional Inventory" check-in felt like extra friction during high-motivation moments. "Ambient Transition Cues" were too subtle to notice during focused work. — [Chen et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.17258v1)
- [PEER] Shame from childhood labels such as "lazy" persisted after diagnosis. — [Chen et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.17258v1)
- [Practitioner content] Constant digital notifications at work make challenges worse. — [Simply Psychology](https://www.simplypsychology.org/adhd-problems-at-work.html)

### Inferences
- The ideal tone is a supportive friend, not a manager or monitor. That fits the "alter ego" framing, but the surveillance quote (S14) is a direct warning for an app that listens to or captures conversations. Users need clear control, visibility into what is stored, and opt-in capture.
- "Comes to me" plus "becomes invisible after a few days" creates a design tension: push is needed, but static, repetitive push habituates. Varying the timing, linking reminders to context (before seeing the person), and phrasing them like a person would may slow habituation.
- Anything that displays failure (overdue counts, broken streaks, empty days) should be avoided by default. "Brain weather"-style capacity framing tested well.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed source testing **"walls of text"** or information density preferences in ND adults. This is likely true from community discourse but was not sourced here.
- The search found no first-person sources on **AI memory or "second brain" tools** for ND users specifically, beyond the Chen et al. concepts.
- The later part of the Chen et al. paper (beyond the first ~100k characters) was not read and may contain more quotes and design implications.

## 5. Why neurodivergent people abandon planners, journals, and productivity apps (the "app graveyard")

### Takeaway
Tools get abandoned because they assume steady self-regulation and linear time, need daily upkeep the user can't sustain, stop working once the novelty fades (reminders "become invisible"), and turn missed days into visible failure records that trigger shame and avoidance. Upkeep itself can become a way to procrastinate. The peer-reviewed evidence is one strong qualitative study. The rest is vendor and community content.

### Cited Findings
- [PEER] Productivity tools built for neurotypical users assume "steady self-regulation and linear time," which overlooks how ADHD works. The authors frame the difficulty as emotional and relational misalignment, "not a lack of willpower." — [Chen et al. 2026, abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17258)
- [PEER] Tools worked only when tied to routines or relationships. Without reinforcement they became "digital clutter." When participants were the only ones reminding themselves, reminders became invisible within days (P11). — [Chen et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.17258v1)
- [PEER] Setting up and maintaining planning tools sometimes became a form of avoidance. P9: "I sometimes spend more time organizing the to-do app than doing the task." — [Chen et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.17258v1)
- [PEER] Survey data cited in the Chen et al. paper found that adults with ADHD rated common tools as less effective than non-ADHD users did. — [Chen et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.17258v1)
- [Secondhand; unverified] A 2022 *Frontiers in Digital Health* study reportedly found that SMS reminders for adults with ADHD increased engagement in week 2 but not after, as habituation set in. This is reported by a medication-app company's blog. I could not verify the original study. — [Wellnest, DEV Community](https://dev.to/wellnestapp/why-most-medication-apps-fail-adhd-brains-3hea)
- [VENDOR] "The novelty fades, the system becomes another low-interest routine." — [Tiimo, "Why productivity systems fail ADHD"](https://www.tiimoapp.com/resource-hub/why-productivity-systems-fail-adhd)
- [Tech journalism] Apps that specifically target people with ADHD are often not meaningfully different from general productivity apps. — [Android Authority](https://www.androidauthority.com/productivity-apps-failed-3639859/)
- [COMMUNITY] Streak breaks drive abandonment: posters describe deleting apps after breaking a 30+ day streak, or quitting habit apps after missing one day. Reddit content seen through third-party mirrors; individual attribution unverified. — [Reddit mirror snapshot (example)](https://reddit.sentinel-team.org/posts/1rn77k0/snapshots/2026-03-13T19%3A10%3A13.708674Z)
- [COMMUNITY/VENDOR] Dated planners create a visible "failure record," with empty boxes serving as reminders of missed days. ### Inferences
- The abandonment cycle is: novelty, then upkeep load, then a missed day, then a visible failure, then shame, then avoidance, then deletion. Twinwire can break it at three points: (1) **zero required upkeep**, where capture is passive or nearly passive; (2) **no failure ledger**, with no streaks, overdue counts, or empty-day displays; (3) **easy re-entry**, so coming back after weeks away is pleasant ("here's what you missed, nothing to clean up").
- Coach Banks's "temporary systems" framing suggests accepting churn instead of fighting it. Memory should persist even if the user stops interacting for a month, so returning never requires catching up.
- Reminders going invisible after a few days lines up closely with the user's "3-day memory fade." Both the person's memory and their self-set reminders seem to decay on a similar timescale. This is an inference from one quote, not a measured finding.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed longitudinal data on app retention or abandonment rates in ADHD or autistic users compared with neurotypical users.
- The 2022 SMS-habituation study could not be verified at the primary source.
- I found no peer-reviewed data specific to autistic adults on abandoning planners or apps.

## 6. Prevalence: how many adults have ADHD or are neurodivergent, and how many are undiagnosed

### Takeaway
About 6.0% of US adults (about 15.5 million) report a current ADHD diagnosis, and more than half were diagnosed as adults. Worldwide, an estimated 2.6% of adults have persistent ADHD (since childhood) and 6.8% have symptomatic ADHD, roughly 140 to 366 million people. About 2.2% of US adults (about 5.4 million) are estimated to be autistic. Long-standing evidence shows most adults with ADHD go untreated, but I could not confirm a reliable current "undiagnosed" percentage.

### Cited Findings

**US adult ADHD (CDC, 2024)**
- [GOV] An estimated 6.0% of US adults (1 in 16, about 15.5 million) had a current ADHD diagnosis. Data: NCHS Rapid Surveys System, October–November 2023. Published in MMWR vol. 73, no. 40, October 10, 2024. — [CDC MMWR 2024](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/73/wr/mm7340a1.htm); [PubMed 39388378](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39388378/)
- [GOV] 55.9% of adults with ADHD were diagnosed in adulthood (age 18+). — [CDC MMWR 2024](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/73/wr/mm7340a1.htm)
- [GOV] About one third took a stimulant in the past year, and 71.5% of those had trouble getting a prescription filled because it was unavailable. About half had ever used telehealth for ADHD-related services. — [CDC MMWR 2024](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/73/wr/mm7340a1.htm)
- [GOV] Caveats: self-reported diagnosis. Earlier national adult estimates relied on 2003 data. — [CDC MMWR 2024](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/73/wr/mm7340a1.htm)
- [PRESS] Coverage: "One in 16 US adults estimated to have ADHD, with more than half diagnosed in adulthood." — [Healio, Oct 2024](https://cm.healio.com/news/psychiatry/20241023/one-in-16-us-adults-estimated-to-have-adhd-with-more-than-half-diagnosed-in-adulthood); [CHADD, General Prevalence of ADHD in Adults](https://chadd.org/about-adhd/general-prevalence-adults/)

**Global adult ADHD (Song et al., 2021)**
- [PEER] Global systematic review and meta-analysis, *Journal of Global Health* (February 2021): **persistent** adult ADHD (with childhood onset) 2.58%, **symptomatic** adult ADHD (no childhood-onset requirement) 6.76%, adjusted to the 2020 global population. That equals about **139.84 million** (persistent) and **366.33 million** (symptomatic) adults worldwide in 2020. Studies that required childhood onset reported lower estimates. — [Song et al. 2021, PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7916320/); [JOGH](https://jogh.org/the-prevalence-of-adult-attention-deficit-hyperactivity-disorder-a-global-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis/)
- [PEER] A later JOGH commentary reports the figures as about 6.8% and 2.6% across 40 datasets and refers to a 2021 demographic adjustment, which differs slightly from the 2020 basis in the original abstract. — [JOGH 2022 commentary](https://jogh.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/06/jogh-12-03024.pdf)

**Undiagnosed / untreated adult ADHD (older, foundational)**
- [PEER/GOV, 2006 — foundational, older] Kessler et al. (2006), National Comorbidity Survey Replication, *American Journal of Psychiatry*: screened 3,199 US adults aged 18–44 and estimated **4.4%** current adult ADHD prevalence. Many respondents with ADHD had been treated for other mental or substance-use disorders, but their ADHD usually went untreated. — [NIMH Science Update 2006 (archived)](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/eot2008/20080916154350/http://www.nimh.nih.gov/science-news/2006/harvard-study-suggests-significant-prevalence-of-adhd-symptoms-among-adults.shtml); [Psychiatric News 2006](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/pn.41.9.0022)
  - **Conflict:** the share who received ADHD treatment in the past year is cited as **10.9%** in some places and **25.2%** in later papers citing the study. I could not resolve this from the sources reached. Check the original tables before citing either figure. — [PMC3383088 (citing paper)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3383088)
- [GOV] The CDC 2024 finding that 55.9% were diagnosed only in adulthood points indirectly to many people going undiagnosed through childhood. — [CDC MMWR 2024](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/73/wr/mm7340a1.htm)
- [PRESS] Masking may delay diagnosis and limit access to support. — [SFU media release 2026](https://www.sfu.ca/sfunews/media/media-releases/2026/06/adults-with-adhd-may-pay-high-price-to-mask-traits-and-fit-in--s.html)

**US adult autism (CDC, Dietz et al., 2020)**
- [GOV/PEER] About 2.2% of US adults (about 5.4 million aged 18+, roughly 1 in 45) estimated to have autism spectrum disorder. Dietz et al., *Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders* (2020). This is a **modeled** estimate, not a measurement: it starts from 2016–2018 National Survey of Children's Health child prevalence and adjusts for mortality and Census data. The authors note there are no psychometrically validated autism tests for adults, and the true number may be higher or lower. — [WTOP/AP coverage, May 2020](https://wtop.com/health-fitness/2020/05/first-us-study-of-autism-in-adults-estimates-2-2-have-autism-spectrum-disorder/); [Disability Scoop](https://disabilityscoop.com/?p=28327)
- [Advocacy opinion] Critics argue the model wrongly assumes autism prevalence by birth year is constant across ages. — [National Council on Severe Autism blog](https://www.ncsautism.org/blog/7g63q1d3zttpx9ye9u8r0hsed6fa8t)

### Inferences
- A conservative US addressable figure for ADHD plus autism is roughly 6% + 2.2% of adults, but the two overlap (AuDHD), so they cannot simply be added. More than half of diagnosed ADHD adults were diagnosed late, which suggests a large group of people diagnosed late who have already built (and abandoned) many coping systems.
- Global symptomatic adult ADHD (about 366 million) supports the "ND-first but usable by everyone" positioning. Many people with subclinical or undiagnosed symptoms have the same memory pain points.

### Gaps
- I found no reliable, current estimate of the share of US or global adults with **undiagnosed** ADHD. The figure often quoted online ("less than 20% of adults with ADHD are diagnosed") was not verified in this search.
- I found no total estimate of **all neurodivergent adults** (the commonly cited "15–20% of the population is neurodivergent" was not sourced here).
- I found no reliable **global adult autism** prevalence or **AuDHD co-occurrence** rate in this search. WHO autism figures were not retrieved.
- The CDC 2024 figure counts **diagnosed** ADHD only and excludes undiagnosed adults.
